How it works

The chromosome

Each finch carries 16 bits: four genes of four bits, so each gene is a number from 0 to 15. That makes 65,536 possible birds. The page scores every one of them, so it knows each island’s ideal bird in advance.

Selection

Each generation the birds are ranked by the island’s fitness formula. The fitter half survive and the rest die. Survivors are paired at random and each pair has two chicks, so the population stays the same size. That is why it goes up in fours.

Breeding and mutation

Single-point crossover: both parents’ chromosomes are cut at the same random point and the tails swapped, giving two chicks. Then each of a chick’s bits has a small chance of flipping. Each island sets its own mutation rate.

Gray code

In plain binary, 7 is 0111 and 8 is 1000: a one-step change in a trait needs all four bits to flip at once, so evolution can stall just short of the best bird (a “Hamming cliff”). In Gray code, neighbouring numbers always differ by one bit. Switch the encoding to see the difference.

Fast and slow islands

Smooth formulas with a high mutation rate evolve fast. Where genes are linked (brain and beak in the rainforest) or there is a false peak (running on Ember Isle), evolution is slower, and small populations can get stuck.

Bigger populations

More birds means more variety in the gene pool, so good combinations turn up sooner and false peaks are easier to escape. Use the sweep in the lab to see it.
